\section*{How To Use This Sheet}
Start with the heuristic problems to complete the Pair 1 curriculum.
The advanced and algorithmic sections then deepen those ideas if time
permits. For algorithmic bridge problems, sketch the algorithm and
argue correctness before writing code.

\section{Wishful Thinking}

\subsection*{Basic -- Complete The Square}
For all real numbers $x$ and $y$, prove that
\[
x^2+y^2\ge \frac{(x+y)^2}{2}.
\]
Try to rewrite the difference as something that is clearly non-negative.

\subsection*{Easy -- Make The Sum Telescope}
Prove that
\[
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k(k+1)}=\frac{n}{n+1}.
\]
Wish that each term were a difference of two simpler fractions.

\subsection*{Medium -- A Number Puzzle}
Construct infinitely many integer solutions to $x^2+y^2=2z^2$ with
$x,y,z>0$. Wish that a Pythagorean triple were hidden in the equation.

\subsection*{Hard -- Improve The Recurrence}
Let $a_1=1$ and $a_{n+1}=2a_n+1$. Find a closed formula for $a_n$.
Wish that the unwanted $+1$ were absorbed into the sequence.

\subsection*{Very Hard -- Factor The Equation}
For a fixed positive integer $n$, find all positive integer pairs
$(x,y)$ satisfying
\[
\frac1x+\frac1y=\frac1n.
\]
Wish that the equation could be written as a product.

\section{Symmetry}

\subsection*{Basic -- Chomp: Poisoned Chocolate}
On an $m\times n$ rectangular chocolate bar, the bottom-left square is
poisoned. Players alternately choose a square and eat it together with
all squares above and to its right. The player forced to eat the
poisoned square loses. Show that the first player has a winning
strategy for any $m,n\ge2$. Use a strategy-stealing argument.

\subsection*{Easy -- Coins On A Circular Table}
Two players alternately place identical non-overlapping circular coins
on a circular table. The player who cannot move loses. Show that the
first player has a winning strategy.

\subsection*{Medium -- A Symmetric Integral}
An integrable function $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ satisfies
$f(x)+f(1-x)=1$ for every $x\in[0,1]$. Evaluate
$\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx$.

\subsection*{Hard -- Odd Skew-Symmetric Matrices}
Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ real matrix satisfying $A^{\mathsf T}=-A$.
Prove that if $n$ is odd, then $\det A=0$.

\subsection*{Very Hard -- Six People}
Among any six people, prove that there are either three mutual
acquaintances or three mutual strangers.

\section{Extreme Principle}

\subsection*{Basic -- Erd\H{o}s--Szekeres Monotone Subsequence}
Prove that any sequence of $n^2+1$ distinct real numbers contains an
increasing or decreasing subsequence of length $n+1$. (Hint: associate
each term with the length of the longest increasing subsequence
starting there.)

\subsection*{Easy -- Minimum Degree Forces A Cycle}
Prove that every finite graph in which every vertex has degree at
least 2 contains a cycle. Start with a longest path.

\subsection*{Medium -- An Empty Triangle}
A finite set of points in the plane is not contained in one line.
Prove that three of the points form a triangle whose interior contains
none of the other points.

\subsection*{Hard -- A Path Through A Tournament}
In a tournament, every pair of vertices is joined by exactly one
directed edge. Prove that every finite tournament has a directed path
that visits every vertex exactly once.

\subsection*{Very Hard -- Longest Paths Meet}
Prove that any two longest paths in a finite connected graph share at
least one vertex.

\section{Reduction}

\subsection*{Basic -- An Acquaintance Graph}
At a party of $n\ge2$ people, acquaintance is mutual. Prove that two
people have the same number of acquaintances at the party. Reduce the
problem to a graph and consider the possible vertex degrees.

\subsection*{Easy -- The Bridges Of K\"onigsberg}
Reduce the bridges puzzle to a graph and prove that no walk crosses
each of the seven bridges exactly once.

\subsection*{Medium -- Missionaries And Cannibals}
Three missionaries and three cannibals must cross a river using a
boat that carries at most two people. On a bank with at least one
missionary, cannibals must never outnumber missionaries. Model the
problem as a state graph and find a safe crossing sequence.

\subsection*{Hard -- Lamps As Linear Algebra}
$n$ lamps are arranged in a circle. A move toggles lamp $i$ and its
two neighbours. Given an initial configuration, give an algorithm to
decide whether all lamps can be turned off.

\subsection*{Very Hard -- Assignment As Maximum Flow}
There are $n$ students and $n$ projects. Each student supplies a list
of acceptable projects. Build a flow network for assigning every
student a distinct acceptable project. State how its maximum flow
determines whether an assignment exists.

\section{Advanced Logic And Common Knowledge}

\subsection*{Basic -- Blue-Eyed Islanders ($k=2$)}
There are 2 islanders with blue eyes, and the rest have brown. They
cannot see their own eyes. A trusted outsider announces: ``At least
one of you has blue eyes.'' Each day, anyone who knows their own eye
colour leaves at midnight. What happens and on which day?

\subsection*{Easy -- Cheating Husbands}
In a village of 100 couples, some husbands have been unfaithful.
Every wife knows which \emph{other} husbands have cheated, but not
her own. The queen announces: ``At least one husband has been
unfaithful.'' If a wife deduces her husband cheated, she must act at
midnight. What happens?

\subsection*{Medium -- Sum And Product Puzzle}
Two integers $x,y$ with $2 \le x < y$ and $x+y \le 100$. Mr P knows
the product $xy$; Mr S knows the sum $x+y$.
P: ``I don't know the numbers.''
S: ``I knew you didn't know.''
P: ``Now I know the numbers.''
S: ``Now I know them too.''
Find $x$ and $y$.

\subsection*{Hard -- Cheryl's Birthday}
Cheryl gives Albert and Bernard this list of 10 possible dates:
May 15, May 16, May 19; June 17, June 18; July 14, July 16;
August 14, August 15, August 17. She whispers the month to Albert
and the day to Bernard.
Albert: ``I don't know the birthday, but I know Bernard doesn't know either.''
Bernard: ``At first I didn't know, but now I do.''
Albert: ``Now I also know.''
When is Cheryl's birthday?

\subsection*{Very Hard -- 100 Prisoners And A Light Bulb}
100 prisoners are in solitary cells. There is a central room with a
light bulb (initially off) and a switch. One prisoner at a time is
chosen independently and uniformly at random to visit the room;
they may toggle the switch and/or
announce ``everyone has visited.'' If correct, all go free; if wrong,
all die. They may agree a strategy beforehand. Design a protocol that
never announces incorrectly and eventually frees everyone with
probability 1.

\section{Advanced Invariants And Monovariants}

\subsection*{Basic -- Peg Solitaire (Small Board)}
On a $3\times3$ board with the centre empty, pegs jump orthogonally
over adjacent pegs into empty squares, removing the jumped peg. Can
you finish with one peg in the centre?

\subsection*{Easy -- Token Game}
Tokens on integer points. A move: choose $x$ with tokens at $x$ and
$x+1$; remove them and place one at $x+2$. Start with one token at 0
and one at 1. Can you ever have a token at 100? How many moves are
possible?

\subsection*{Medium -- Conway's Soldiers}
On an infinite checkerboard with $x,y\in\mathbb Z$, pegs occupy all
squares with $y \le 0$.
A peg jumps orthogonally over another into an empty square, removing
the jumped peg. Can a peg ever reach $y=5$? (Hint: for a target
$(x_0,5)$, use weights $\varphi^{|x-x_0|+|y-5|}$ where
$\varphi=(\sqrt5-1)/2$.)

\subsection*{Hard -- Polynomial Invariant For (x+2,y-1)}
A token starts at $(0,0)$ on the integer grid $\mathbb Z^2$. A move
changes $(x,y)$ to $(x+2,y-1)$.
Find a non-constant polynomial $P(x,y)$ such that $P(x+2,y-1)=P(x,y)$
for all $x,y$. Characterise all reachable positions using this
invariant.

\subsection*{Very Hard -- A Sequence Invariant}
Given integer sequence $a_0, a_1, \ldots$ with $a_0 = 0$, $a_1 = 1$,
and $a_{n+1} = a_n + a_{n-1} + 1$ for $n\ge1$, prove that
\[
(-1)^{n-1}\bigl((a_n+1)^2-(a_{n-1}+1)(a_{n+1}+1)\bigr)=1
\quad(n\ge1).
\]

\section{Colouring And Tiling: Generalisations}

\subsection*{Basic -- Mutilated Chessboard: Four Corners Removed}
An $8\times8$ chessboard has all four corner squares removed. Can the
remaining board be tiled by dominoes? If so, give a tiling.

\subsection*{Easy -- Tiling With T-Tetrominoes}
Tile a $4\times4$ board by T-tetrominoes (four squares in a T shape).
Extend your construction to every $4\times(4k)$ board, $k\ge1$.

\subsection*{Medium -- Fault-Free Domino Tiling}
A domino tiling of a rectangle has a fault line if an internal
horizontal or vertical grid line from one side to the other cuts no
dominoes. Prove that a $6\times6$ board has no fault-free tiling,
but a $6\times8$ board does.

\subsection*{Hard -- Triominoes On A Chessboard}
Can an $8\times8$ chessboard with one corner removed be tiled by
straight trominoes ($1\times3$ rectangles)? Use a 3-colouring of the board.

\subsection*{Very Hard -- Generalised Mutilated Chessboard}
For which positive $m,n$ can an $m\times n$ board \emph{always} be
tiled by dominoes after any two squares of opposite chessboard colours
are removed? An empty tiling is allowed. Prove your characterisation.

\section{Induction And Recursion: From Proof To Algorithm}

\subsection*{Basic -- Josephus Problem}
$n$ people stand in a circle. Eliminate every second person until one
remains. Where should you stand to survive? Find a recurrence and
closed form for $J(n)$.

\subsection*{Easy -- Gray Codes}
An $n$-bit Gray code lists all $2^n$ binary strings so consecutive
strings differ in one bit. Give a recursive construction. Prove it
works by induction.

\subsection*{Medium -- Recursive 15-Puzzle Parity}
Given a $4\times4$ 15-puzzle configuration, design a recursive
algorithm to determine if it is solvable. Use the invariant from Week 1.

\subsection*{Hard -- Counterfeit Coin With $n$ Weighings}
Generalise the 12-coin, 3-weighing problem. With $n\ge2$ weighings on a
balance scale, what is the maximum number of coins among which you
can find one counterfeit (heavy or light)? Give a strategy achieving the bound.

\subsection*{Very Hard -- Hadamard Matrix Construction}
A Hadamard matrix of order $n$ is an $n\times n$ matrix with entries
$\pm1$ whose rows are mutually orthogonal. Give a recursive
construction for $n=2^k$ and prove it works.

\section{Algorithmic Bridges}

\subsection*{Basic -- A Maze As A Graph}
You are given a rectangular grid of open and blocked cells, together
with a start and finish cell. You may move one cell up, down, left, or
right through open cells. Model the maze as a graph, describe how BFS
finds the minimum number of moves, and explain why the answer is optimal.

\subsection*{Easy -- Greedy Activity Selection}
You have $n$ half-open intervals $[s_i, f_i)$ with $s_i<f_i$.
Choose a maximum-size subset of non-overlapping intervals. Prove
that picking the interval with
earliest finish time, then recursing, is optimal. (Exchange argument.)

\subsection*{Medium -- DP From Induction: Coin Change}
Given coin values $c_1, \ldots, c_k$ and target $n$, find the minimum
number of coins to make exactly $n$. Define $f(n)$ recursively,
identify overlapping subproblems, and give a tabulation algorithm.

\subsection*{Hard -- Sliding Puzzle State-Space Search}
$2\times3$ sliding puzzle with tiles 1--5 and one empty. From a given
start state, find the minimum moves to reach the goal. Describe the
state encoding, transitions, and why BFS works. (There are 360
reachable states from any fixed goal.)

\subsection*{Very Hard -- Greedy Scheduling With Deadlines}
$n$ jobs, each takes 1 unit of time, has an integer deadline $d_i\ge1$
and positive profit $p_i$. You may schedule at most one job per time
slot. A job only earns profit if completed by its deadline. Design a greedy algorithm
to maximise total profit and prove it correct. (Hint: consider jobs
in decreasing profit order; use a disjoint-set union to find latest free slot.)
